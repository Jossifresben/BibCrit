# Peshitta Correspondences: methodology and sources

This document explains how the Peshitta Correspondences workbench (`/translation-technique` in BibCrit) produces its numbers, which published methods and resources it rests on, and where its limits are. It is written for scholars who want to check the instrument rather than take its output on trust. The operational details (scripts, file formats, rebuild order) are in [translation-technique.md](translation-technique.md); the design spec is in `docs/superpowers/specs/2026-10-07-translation-technique-design.md`.

## 1. The question

Translation-technique study asks how a translator rendered a source text: which target lexemes correspond to a given source lexeme, how consistently, and what conditions the choice. For the Septuagint this programme goes back to Aejmelaeus and Soisalon-Soininen; for the Peshitta, Weitzman's *The Syriac Version of the Old Testament* (1999) set the frame, and the ETCBC projects on Kings, Judges and Ezra (CALAP, Turgama) built clause-level synopses of Hebrew and Syriac by hand. Logan Copley's dissertation on lexical consistency in the Peshitta of Deuteronomy, presented in October 2026, encoded Deuteronomy in that tradition and studied five verbs in depth; its findings for בוא, נתן and ירד are the published figures this tool is checked against.

The workbench does one thing those studies did by hand: it builds the Hebrew–Syriac word correspondence for a whole book or the whole Old Testament by computation, and then counts. It makes no claim about why a translator chose a rendering. That remains the scholar's.

## 2. Hard rules

- **No language model produces a number.** Every count, share, cross-tabulation and statistic is computed by deterministic code over a table of rows. A model is used only to *annotate* (propose a lemma for a form the lexicon cannot resolve; adjudicate an alignment link the statistical method left uncertain), and every such annotation is tagged with its source so the share of a table that rests on it is always displayed.
- **No model at query time.** The web page calls no model. All model calls happen offline in build scripts, and their outputs are versioned data.
- **Everything is reproducible.** The data carries a version, a build manifest, and a SHA-256 hash of the alignment files; the page prints the hash. The same inputs and scripts give the same files.

## 3. The Hebrew side

Hebrew text, lexemes and grammar come from the **ETCBC BHSA** database (Biblia Hebraica Stuttgartensia Amstelodamensis), read through **Text-Fabric**. Lexeme identifiers (`JRD[`, `BW>[`), parts of speech, verbal stems and tenses, clause types and phrase functions are BHSA's own, which also makes them the identifiers used in SHEBANQ and MQL queries. The conditioning facets on the page are these BHSA features of the Hebrew occurrence: stem (`vs`), tense (`vt`), clause type (`typ`), whether the clause has an explicit object phrase (phrase `function` = `Objc`), and the preposition following the verb within its clause. Facet values are shown with their BHSA codes and decoded names.

## 4. The Syriac side: the lemma layer

The Peshitta text is the **ETCBC Peshitta** module (Leiden text, Text-Fabric edition), which carries surface forms but no lemmas. The lemma layer is built in three tiers, and every token records which tier produced its lemma:

1. **SEDRA IV** (Beth Mardutho's Syriac Electronic Data Research Archive, by George Kiraz and collaborators), queried by surface form after stripping seyame and diacritic dots. A form with one analysis is resolved outright; a form with several keeps all candidates and is resolved by the alignment (§5.4).
2. **Affix rules** on a lexicon miss: proclitic ܘ, ܕ, ܠ, ܒ are stripped in turn, then pronominal suffixes, and the stripped form is looked up again. These rules are the kind every Syriac grammar states (e.g. Nöldeke, *Compendious Syriac Grammar*, §§ on proclitics and suffixes); they are heuristics and are tagged as such.
3. **Model proposal** for what remains, in batches, with strict parsing: a proposed lemma must be Syriac script, its part of speech must come from SEDRA's category set, and anything else is rejected rather than repaired. Tagged `model`.

For Deuteronomy the shares are about 81% SEDRA, 12% rules, 7% model, 0.1% unresolved. One known limit: a proposal is made per surface form, so homographs share one answer.

## 5. The alignment

### 5.1 The method

Word alignment uses **IBM Model 1**, the first of the statistical translation models of Brown, Della Pietra, Della Pietra and Mercer (1993). It treats each Hebrew–Syriac verse pair as a bag of words, assumes every Syriac token was generated by exactly one Hebrew word (or by an invisible "null" word), and estimates a translation table *t*(syriac lemma | hebrew lexeme) by expectation–maximization over the whole corpus: start from uniform probabilities, compute for each Syriac token how its probability mass splits over the Hebrew words of its verse, re-estimate the table from those fractional counts, repeat. Five iterations are run. A small smoothing constant is added to the denominators so that a word seen once cannot look certain.

Model 1 ignores word order, which is why Brown et al. went on to Models 2–5. Two additions compensate here:

- **A diagonal prior.** Links between words at similar relative positions in the verse get a mild preference, following the idea of Dyer, Chahuneau and Smith's *fast_align* (2013). The Peshitta follows Hebrew word order closely enough that this is a strong and cheap signal.
- **Two directions, symmetrized.** A second table is trained from Syriac to Hebrew. Links chosen by both directions are accepted; links chosen by one direction are added only if they are adjacent to an accepted link, leave at least one endpoint free, and score above a threshold on the geometric mean of the two directional posteriors. This is the *grow-diag* family of heuristics from Och and Ney (2003) and Koehn, Och and Marcu (2003), with the two-sided score as an extra guard. The guard is what prevents a token with no true counterpart (the Peshitta's "enemy" at Deut 22:4, where the Hebrew has "brother") from being attached to a nearby word.

### 5.2 Why this method and not a newer one

Neural and embedding-based aligners exist and are more accurate on general text. Model 1 was chosen because it is transparent: every link carries a probability that came from counting co-occurrences in these verses, the training can be rerun by anyone in minutes, and there is no learned component whose behaviour cannot be inspected. For a translation that tracks its source as closely as the Peshitta does, the gap in accuracy is small; for a tool whose purpose is to let scholars check every number, the gap in auditability is decisive.

### 5.3 What a link is

A row in the alignment table is one link: a Hebrew word (BHSA node), a Syriac token (verse position), the symmetrized probability, and a kind: `one-one`, `one-many` (one Hebrew word rendered by several tokens), `many-one` (several Hebrew words by one token), or `null` (no counterpart on one side). Counts on the page are counts of links, not of Hebrew occurrences: a Hebrew word aligned to two Syriac tokens contributes two rows. The page says so.

### 5.4 Ambiguous lemmas

A Syriac token with several SEDRA analyses takes the candidate best supported by the Hebrew words it is linked to, summed over its links, and that choice is written back into the lemma layer with the posterior as its confidence.

### 5.5 Model adjudication

Links whose probability falls below 0.3 are listed as pending. A model may be asked, per link, which token in the verse renders the Hebrew word, or none. Answers naming a position outside the verse are rejected. Accepted answers replace the statistical link and are tagged `model`; their rows carry no probability, because a model's say-so is not a posterior. For Deuteronomy, 730 links were adjudicated by `claude-opus-5-5`; the manifest names the model.

## 6. The counts and statistics

- **Distribution.** For a Hebrew lexeme and a selection of books, the Syriac lemmas it is linked to, with counts, the number of occurrences with no counterpart, and the share of links that rest on model-proposed lemmas or model-adjudicated links.
- **Cross-tabulation.** Syriac lemma against a facet value. Association is tested with Pearson's chi-square test of independence, and effect size reported as Cramér's V (Cramér, *Mathematical Methods of Statistics*, 1946). When any expected cell count is below 5, the chi-square approximation is unreliable (Cochran's rule) and the page shows only that warning, with no statistics. V is glossed as negligible (< 0.1), weak (< 0.3), moderate (< 0.5) or strong.
- **Findings line.** A short summary generated by code from the table: dominant rendering and its share, number of distinct renderings, null share, model share, and the facet with the strongest reliable association. It makes no causal claim and is labelled "not an interpretation".
- **Lexical consistency.** For each part of speech, a histogram of how many Hebrew lexemes have one, two, three… distinct Syriac renderings. This is the corpus-level view of the dissertation's question.

## 7. Witnesses

The ETCBC Peshitta text is the Leiden main text. The Leiden apparatus is not open data and is not shipped. The workbench has a witness layer in which readings can be keyed by hand, each with a mandatory note of where it was keyed from; a reading can replace a token's lemma or, where the main text has no counterpart for a Hebrew word, assert the link (as 9a1's "brother" does at Deut 22:4). Every table can be rerun for a witness, and substituted rows are tagged `witness`.

## 8. Evaluation

The aligner's accuracy is measured against a gold sample: 200 Deuteronomy verses aligned by hand by the project owner, with a model as an independent second reader; precision and recall are computed on the links both readers agree on, and the disagreement count is published beside them. Until the sample exists the page says "unevaluated" and every count is provisional.

A second check is the dissertation's published figures for Deuteronomy. At the time of writing: ירד to ܢܚܬ 9 of 9 and to ܟܒܫ 2 of 2 agree; the dissertation's one ܐܬܐ at Deut 1:25 is mis-linked by the aligner with high confidence. For נתן the tool counts 124 ܝܗܒ against the dissertation's ~133, because the dissertation merges the suppletive pair ܝܗܒ/ܢܬܠ and the aligner leaves 32 occurrences unlinked that the scholar resolved by hand. These are reported as findings, not tuned away.

## 9. Limits

- The lemma layer's rule and model tiers are heuristics; their shares are shown, not hidden.
- Model 1 is weak on free renderings and idioms; those links get low probabilities and stay visible in the occurrence list.
- Facets are limited to what BHSA encodes. Animacy, telicity and other semantic properties are not available and would need a separate, scholar-validated annotation layer.
- Books other than Deuteronomy have, at present, no model adjudication: their unresolved forms and weak links are what the lexicon and the statistics could do alone.
